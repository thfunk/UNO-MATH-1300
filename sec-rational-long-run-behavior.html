    Section 8.3 Long-Run Behavior and Horizontal Asymptotes

    Rational functions often approach a predictable value as the input becomes very large in magnitude. This long-run behavior is closely connected to the degrees and leading coefficients of the numerator and denominator. By comparing those features, we can determine whether a rational function has a horizontal asymptote and describe its end behavior without graphing every detail.
    🔗
    This section focuses on behavior far to the left and far to the right. A rational graph may have intercepts, breaks, or dramatic changes in the middle, but those features do not determine its long-run behavior.
    🔗

    Subsection 8.3.1 Reading Long-Run Behavior

    The notation \(x\to\infty\) describes inputs moving far to the right, while \(x\to-\infty\) describes inputs moving far to the left. If the output approaches a fixed value \(c\text{,}\) we write \(f(x)\to c\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 8.3.1. Interpreting End-Behavior Notation.

    Interpret the statement
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x)\to-4 \text{ as } x\to\infty. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    As the input becomes increasingly large and positive, the outputs of the function become increasingly close to \(-4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    This statement does not say that the function must equal \(-4\) for a particular input. It describes a trend in the outputs as the inputs continue increasing.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.3.2 Horizontal Asymptotes

    Definition 8.3.2. Horizontal Asymptote.

    A horizontal line \(y=c\) is a horizontal asymptote of a function if the outputs approach \(c\) as \(x\to\infty\text{,}\) as \(x\to-\infty\text{,}\) or in both directions.
    🔗
    🔗
    A horizontal asymptote summarizes long-run behavior. It does not act as a barrier that the graph is forbidden to cross. The graph may cross its horizontal asymptote at a finite input and still approach that line farther to the left or right.
    🔗

    Example 8.3.3. Reading an Asymptote from End Behavior.

    A function satisfies
    🔗
    \begin{align*} f(x)&\to1 \text{ as } x\to-\infty,\\ f(x)&\to1 \text{ as } x\to\infty. \end{align*}
    Identify its horizontal asymptote.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The outputs approach 1 at both ends of the graph, so the horizontal asymptote is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This example shows the direct connection between a limiting output and a horizontal asymptote. The long-run statement and the asymptote describe the same behavior in two different forms.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.3.3 Comparing the Degrees of the Numerator and Denominator

    For a rational function \(f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\text{,}\) the highest-degree terms control the long-run behavior. The lower-degree terms become less important relative to those leading terms when \(|x|\) is very large.
    🔗
    1. If \(\deg(P)<\deg(Q)\text{,}\) then the horizontal asymptote is \(y=0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(\deg(P)=\deg(Q)\text{,}\) then the horizontal asymptote is the ratio of the leading coefficients.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. If \(\deg(P)>\deg(Q)\text{,}\) then there is no horizontal asymptote.
      🔗
      🔗
    These cases are based on which polynomial grows faster. When the denominator has the larger degree, it outgrows the numerator and drives the ratio toward zero. When the degrees match, the leading terms grow at the same rate, so their coefficient ratio determines the limiting value. When the numerator has the larger degree, the ratio does not level off toward a constant.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.3.4 Numerator Degree Less Than Denominator Degree

    When the denominator has the larger degree, the denominator grows faster than the numerator. The fraction becomes closer and closer to zero as the magnitude of \(x\) increases.
    🔗

    Example 8.3.4. A Horizontal Asymptote at y = 0.

    Determine the horizontal asymptote and end behavior of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x)=\frac{3x-2}{x^2+5}. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    The numerator has degree 1, and the denominator has degree 2. Since \(1<2\text{,}\) the denominator grows faster.
    🔗
    The horizontal asymptote is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=0. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} f(x)&\to0 \text{ as } x\to-\infty,\\ f(x)&\to0 \text{ as } x\to\infty. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This example illustrates that the exact coefficients and lower-degree terms do not affect the horizontal asymptote in this case. The comparison of degrees is enough.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.3.5 Equal Numerator and Denominator Degrees

    When the numerator and denominator have the same degree, their leading terms grow at the same rate. Dividing those leading terms leaves the ratio of their leading coefficients.
    🔗

    Example 8.3.5. Using the Leading Coefficients.

    Determine the horizontal asymptote and end behavior of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} g(x)=\frac{5x^2-3x+7}{-2x^2+4}. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Both the numerator and denominator have degree 2. Their leading coefficients are 5 and \(-2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The horizontal asymptote is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=\frac{5}{-2}=-\frac52. \end{equation*}
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} g(x)&\to-\frac52 \text{ as } x\to-\infty,\\ g(x)&\to-\frac52 \text{ as } x\to\infty. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This example shows why the leading coefficients must include their signs. A negative leading coefficient in the denominator produces a negative long-run ratio.
    🔗

    Example 8.3.6. Equal Degrees in Factored Form.

    Determine the horizontal asymptote of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} h(x)=\frac{3(x+4)(x-2)}{2(x-1)(x+7)}. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Both numerator and denominator have degree 2. Their leading coefficients are 3 and 2, so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y=\frac32 \end{equation*}
    is the horizontal asymptote.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    This example demonstrates that expanding is unnecessary. The leading coefficient of each product can be read by multiplying the leading coefficients of its factors.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.3.6 Numerator Degree Greater Than Denominator Degree

    When the numerator has the larger degree, the numerator grows faster than the denominator. The ratio does not approach a finite constant, so there is no horizontal asymptote.
    🔗

    Example 8.3.7. No Horizontal Asymptote.

    Determine whether
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} r(x)=\frac{2x^3-x+1}{x^2+4} \end{equation*}
    has a horizontal asymptote.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The numerator has degree 3, and the denominator has degree 2. Because \(3>2\text{,}\) there is no horizontal asymptote.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    This conclusion does not mean the graph has no predictable long-run pattern. It means only that the graph does not approach a horizontal line. Other asymptotic behavior may be studied later.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 8.3.7 A Complete Long-Run Analysis

    A reliable analysis begins by finding the degree and leading coefficient of both the numerator and denominator. The comparison then identifies the correct horizontal-asymptote case.
    🔗
    1. Find the degree of the numerator.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the degree of the denominator.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Compare the degrees.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Determine the horizontal asymptote, if one exists.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Write the end behavior using \(x\to\infty\) and \(x\to-\infty\) notation.
      🔗
      🔗

    Example 8.3.8. From Equation to End Behavior.

    Determine the long-run behavior of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F(x)=\frac{-6x^3+2x-9}{4x^3+x^2-5}. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    The numerator and denominator both have degree 3. The ratio of their leading coefficients is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{-6}{4}=-\frac32. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the horizontal asymptote is \(y=-\frac32\text{,}\) and
    🔗
    \begin{align*} F(x)&\to-\frac32 \text{ as } x\to-\infty,\\ F(x)&\to-\frac32 \text{ as } x\to\infty. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This example summarizes the main method of the section. The equal degrees identify the correct case, the leading coefficients determine the asymptote, and the asymptote determines the long-run output in both directions.
    🔗
    🔗
    The long-run behavior of a rational function is controlled by the leading terms of its numerator and denominator. If the numerator degree is smaller, the horizontal asymptote is \(y=0\text{.}\) If the degrees are equal, the horizontal asymptote is the ratio of the leading coefficients. If the numerator degree is larger, no horizontal asymptote exists. These degree comparisons provide an efficient way to describe rational functions far from the origin.
